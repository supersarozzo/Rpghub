How to roleplay Skippy

Pirates, half-orcs, dragonborn, sea luck, and old Sicilian and Italian luck customs, all in plain English. A guideline, not a rulebook. Works offline. Prints on paper.

What this page is: ideas for how Skippy talks, acts and believes. Roleplay has no wrong answers. Take what is fun and drop the rest. PHB = Player's Handbook. All book facts are in my own words, with printed page numbers.

Source labels (read this first)

Your books comes from your Player's Handbook or Tasha's, with a page number.
Not your books is D&D lore I know from other books (Monster Manual, Forgotten Realms, Ghosts of Saltmarsh and more) or from memory. I did not check it against a page you own. Ask your DM before you rely on it.
Real world is real history or real folk custom, checked on the web. Most web pages about folk customs are blogs, so details change from family to family.
Made up is my idea for Skippy. It is not in any book. You can change it.

Skippy in one look

A half-orc Artillerist artificer, level 3. A former powder monkey (the boy who runs gunpowder to the cannons) on the pirate ship The Widow. His captain, Edwine Blackscale (a bronze dragonborn), raised him. The ship sank. He is searching for her. He keeps his word like treasure. He hums the old songs of The Widow's crew. His cannon, Mourning Bell, is a small black iron bell-shaped cannon. It has no legs and is Tiny. He fires it from his shoulder like a rocket launcher, or sets it down as a turret. It tolls for the lost crew. STORY His lucky charm is a small red coral horn charm on a cord around his neck.

His heart: loyal (chaotic neutral). A crew is a family. He will break a law, but never a promise. His flaw: he cannot stop looking at a blank map.

The big idea of this page: Skippy lost his ship, his captain and his crew. The old customs are how he holds on to them. His luck rituals are not silly to him. They are memory. Play them with a straight face and a small smile.

1. Pirates in D&D 5e

What your background gives, how ship life works, and how pirates act at the table.

What the Pirate background gives you in play

Your books The Sailor background has a Pirate variant. Its feature is Bad Reputation. PHB p.139 In my words: in towns and cities, ordinary people are scared of you. They let you get away with small crimes, and they do not want to cross you. It is made for small trouble, not for serious crimes.

How Bad Reputation shows up at the table

  • Before a scene, ask the DM: "Does anyone here know The Widow?" This is the way to use the feature. It is a story tool, not a dice roll.
  • Good side: a guard looks away. A shopkeeper stops haggling. A bully backs off.
  • Bad side: a bounty hunter, an enemy captain, or a mother who lost a son at sea may also know the name. This gives the DM story hooks, so it is a gift.
  • Do not: try to use it for theft of big things, murder, or burning a building. The feature is for minor trouble.

One line: "I lean on the counter and say nothing. The Widow's name does the talking."

Ship life

Real world Not your books Real pirate ships of the 1700s ran in simple ways. You can borrow these for Skippy's memories.

Part of ship lifeWhat it wasSkippy's version
Captain / skipperLed in battle. On many pirate ships the crew could vote to remove a bad captain.Edwine, "the skipper". Booming, generous, vain, told terrible jokes.
QuartermasterKept order and shared out the loot.Ask your DM if a quartermaster of The Widow survived. A good ally.

Watches and bellsThe day was split into watches. A ship's bell marked the time.He still counts the bells in his head. A bell ringing makes him look up.
SharesLoot was split by agreed shares, not by the captain alone.He always asks: "And the crew's share?" It is why he is fair at the party's treasure pile.

The pirate code

Real world Real pirate crews wrote "articles" (rules) that every sailor swore to. Typical rules: fair shares, no fighting aboard, a vote for big choices, and money for a sailor hurt in a fight. This is why pirates were not just thieves in the story: they had a code. Famous example: the articles of the pirate Bartholomew Roberts. I know it from memory, not checked now.

The Widow's Articles Made up

Six short rules Skippy can quote. They fit his traits. Show them to your DM.

  1. No one is left behind. (His ideal. He keeps this even now.)
  2. A promise given is a debt owed. (He keeps his word like treasure.)
  3. The powder is for the guns, not for fools. (He is careful with fire and powder.)
  4. The skipper's word is the tide. (He is loyal to Edwine.)
  5. The bell is rung for every one we lose. (Fits Mourning Bell.)
  6. A name is not sold. (She gave him "Skippy". He will never drop it.)

One line: "Article two. I said I would carry it. So I carry it."

How pirates talk and act (without the cartoon)

  • Short words. Crews shouted over wind and guns. Skippy says things plainly: "Aye." "Hold." "Fire." "Down."
  • A few sea words are enough: aye (yes), avast (stop), belay that (forget that order), shipmate (crew friend), landlubber (someone who is not a sailor), fair winds (goodbye and good luck).
  • Loyal inside, wary outside. Pirates trust the crew. They distrust officers, nobles and the law.
  • Stories are treasure. Skippy trades a song or a tale for a favour.
  • A "yarr" now and then is fun. Do not say it every sentence. It gets tiring for the table.
Books you do not own: Ghosts of Saltmarsh (sea adventures and ship rules), the Sword Coast Adventurer's Guide and the Forgotten Realms books have pirate lore and ship fights. I know them from memory, not from your books. If your DM runs a sea game, ask them what they use.

2. Half-orcs as pirates

Your looks, your gods, and how other people see you.

What the book says

Your books The half-orc section is on PHB p.40-41. In my words: half-orcs look strong and rough. Many people fear them, or look down on them, before they know them. Some half-orcs grow up in orc tribes. Others grow up among humans. The half-orc game traits are darkvision (see in the dark), Menacing (a bonus on Intimidation), Relentless Endurance (stay at 1 HP once a day when you would drop to 0) and Savage Attacks (extra damage on a critical hit). PHB p.41 Check your sheet to see which of these Skippy kept.

Skippy is a bit different. He never knew his parents. He grew up on a ship with dragonborn, so he speaks Draconic and has forgotten most of his Orc. He looks like an orc, talks like a dragonborn sailor, and belongs to The Widow. That mix is the best roleplay hook you have.

How others see him

The frightened

Villagers step back. Guards watch his hands. This is the Bad Reputation at work.

One line: He smiles and says: "I only bite on Tuesdays." (Then he checks it is not Tuesday.)

The surprised

People expect a brute. He pulls out a chart and a spyglass. They do not know what to say.

One line: "Ah, you thought I'd smash it. I'm going to measure it."

Gruumsh and the orc gods

Not your books In D&D, orcs honour Gruumsh, the one-eyed god of war and storms. He has a wife, Luthic (hearth, caves, healing), and other gods in the orc pantheon: Bahgtru (his strong son), Ilneval (war planner), Yurtrus (death) and Shargaas (night and stealth). The gods are named in the PHB appendix of gods, but I did not check the page in your copy. The stories about them come from other books. They also say Gruumsh is cruel and hates elves.

A half-orc raised away from orcs does not have to follow Gruumsh. Here are three ways to play it. Pick one:

  • He turned away. He never heard the orc gods at all. The sea was his only church. (Easy. No rules to learn.)
  • He leans to Luthic. The quiet one. Home, healing, a fire in a cave. He lights a small flame for the lost crew. Fits an artificer who fixes things and keeps people alive.
  • He answers to the sea. He fears Umberlee (section 4) and makes a small offering before every voyage. Fear is part of his faith.
Ask your DM first. Your world may not use these gods. Say "Skippy has heard of the orc war-god, and does not follow him." That is true in every world.

Orc sayings and scars as honour

Made up There are no official orc proverbs in the books I know. Here are some I invented. They sound like Skippy's orc blood talking, and he half-remembers them:

"A scar is a story you survived."
"Fire first, talk after. But never lie to a fire."
"The strong carry the loud, the loud carry the small." (He carried the small once: it was him.)

Scars as honour: many orcs in D&D treat scars as proof of courage. Not your books Skippy can wear this too. Give him one or two small burn scars from powder flash on his hands and forearms. When he is asked about them: "Powder monkey. The cannon always bites back." Do not make it a sad moment unless you want to.

3. Dragonborn and Captain Blackscale

For Edwine, the bronze dragonborn who raised him. Skippy learned her ways.

What the book says

Your books Dragonborn are on PHB p.32-34. In my words: dragonborn are proud. Clan matters more to them than life itself. They care a lot about honour, and they dislike showing weakness. They descend from dragons, so each one has a draconic ancestry (a colour of dragon). That decides their breath weapon and damage resistance. A bronze dragonborn has lightning. PHB p.34 Edwine's breath is a line of lightning. It makes sense for a captain: it crosses a deck.

Clan honour on a pirate ship

A dragonborn is proud of her clan name. A pirate gives up the old name and takes a new one. Blackscale is not her clan name. It is a ship name. This is a good question for your DM: what was Edwine's clan, and did she leave it, or was she cast out? If she left it, she may have made The Widow's crew her new clan. That explains why she never left a crew member behind (STORY). And it explains why Skippy feels he is in her clan.

Draconic words Skippy knows Made up

Skippy speaks the language of the crew. Add a Draconic touch, but do not invent a full language. The easy way: say it in English, then say "as the skipper would say" and a word like honour, fire, or home in a growly voice.

One line: "The skipper said it in the old tongue. It means: the clan is the sea under your feet."

Bahamut and Tiamat

Not your books In D&D, dragonborn look up to two great dragon gods. Bahamut, the Platinum Dragon, is a god of justice and good. Tiamat, the five-headed Queen of Dragons, is a goddess of greed and evil. Many dragonborn pick a side. A pirate captain is in between: she wants gold (like Tiamat), but she keeps her crew safe (like Bahamut). I know this from memory and web, not from your two books. The PHB appendix of gods names them. I did not check the page.

  • Edwine might offer a bronze coin to Bahamut before a fight. A bronze coin: it matches her scales.
  • Skippy was not born into this faith. He has her habit, not her creed. He may say: "The skipper prayed to the silver one. I only prayed to her."
  • Some dragonborn sailors who knew her may be devoted to one of these two. That is a way to meet them: a believer in Tiamat blames her for the wreck. A believer in Bahamut helps him.

Bronze dragons love the sea

Not your books In the Monster Manual (not yours) bronze dragons live by coasts. They love ships and the sea. They like to watch navies, and they are known as friendly to humans and to people who protect the coast. That fits Edwine: a bronze dragonborn who chose a ship over land. It is a good detail for her old crew: "She was never seasick. A bronze never is."

Example: A dragonborn sailor in a tavern sees Skippy hum. She stops: "That's an old bronze-crew song." Skippy freezes. This is how the DM can give you a clue about the captain, and it costs nothing.

4. Sea myths and superstitions

What sailors believed. Pick two or three for Skippy. More than that is too many.

Real world Sailors in real history lived in danger and could not control the sea, so they had many luck rules. The web sources say the details change from place to place, and some origin stories are only folk tales.

No whistling on deck

Sailors thought whistling could call up a wind, or even a storm. (The ship's cook was allowed to: if his mouth was whistling, it was not eating the crew's food.)

At the table: Skippy hums but never whistles. If a friend whistles on a boat: "Stop. You'll wake the wind."

Never rename a ship

A named ship was thought to have its own spirit. Changing the name was seen as cheating the sea. (A plain reason: ships had reputations in port by name.)

At the table: "My name is the only thing the skipper gave me. I'll never change it. And you don't rename a boat." (A link between Skippy and his ship.)

The ship's bell and the dead

Bells marked time, marked ceremonies, and were tied to death at sea. Accounts differ: a bell that rings by itself may warn of death, and a bell may drive away evil. Both are folk beliefs.

At the table: When someone falls or an NPC dies, Skippy taps Mourning Bell once with his knuckle. A small, quiet sound. One tap per person lost. It fits the cannon's name.

Coins for the drowned

A coin is put under the mast when a ship is built, for luck. A story links it to paying the ferryman of the dead. That link is a story, not proven history.

At the table: Skippy keeps one old coin from The Widow. Before a sea trip, he presses it to the mast. "For the ones who didn't make it to shore."

Sailing on Friday

Friday was an unlucky day to start a voyage. Sicilian sayings agree (section 5).

At the table: If the party plans to leave on a Friday: "Could we go at dawn on Saturday?" A low-cost line.

Davy Jones' locker

A pirate name for the bottom of the sea, where the drowned go.

At the table: "She's in Davy's locker," he says. Then: "No. She isn't. I'd feel it." (His hope, in one line.)

Fantasy versions

Not your books In the Forgotten Realms (a D&D world, not in your books), the sea goddess is Umberlee. Sailors fear her more than love her. Her titles include "the Wavemother" and "Queen of the Sea". Sailors make offerings to her before a voyage to calm her. Her priests are few and are known to ask a price for safe passage. Most of this is on fan wikis, so details differ. Ask your DM before using her.

  • If your game is in the Realms: Skippy drops a small thing overboard before sailing (a coin, a crumb of ship's biscuit). "A tip for the Wavemother."
  • If it is not: call her "the sea queen" or "the Drowner", or just "her". The gesture is the same.
  • Other sea fears: krakens, sirens, ghost ships, sailors who never came home. The DM may put one in your path. Your fear of the sea can be funny: "I'm not afraid. I'm respectful."
One thing to watch: a superstition is only fun when it is short. Do it once per scene, in a few seconds. The table should never wait for Skippy to finish his ritual.

5. Luck customs from Sicily and Italy

Old sailors' luck customs that fit a sea-going gunner. All in plain English. Each has one line you can use.

Where they come from: Sicily is the big island at the toe of Italy. Its sea culture is very old: fishermen, tuna nets, saints of the sea. These customs are real. Skippy does the actions, and he never needs a foreign word.
How to bring it into your game: Skippy does not say "Sicily". If someone asks, he says "an old sailors' custom" or "where I learned it". Treat these as real traditions of real people: respectful, light, never mocking. UK note: in the UK people knock on wood for luck. Skippy touches iron instead, and his cannon is black iron. That small difference is a nice, memorable habit.

Salt thrown over the shoulder

Real world If salt spills, you take a pinch and throw it over your left shoulder. Web sources say this is Italian and wider European, not only Sicilian. They give two reasons: salt used to be costly, and the pinch is thrown at the devil who stands behind you.

At the table: a tavern cup spills salt. Skippy takes a pinch, throws it left over his shoulder, says: "Done. He's blind for a minute."

The horns sign and the red horn charm

Real world The horns sign is a hand sign: the index finger and little finger up, the other fingers closed. A small red horn charm is worn or kept in a pocket against the evil eye (next card). A web source says it should be a gift, not something you buy for yourself. Warning: pointing the horns sign at a person can be a rude sign. Skippy only makes it down, toward the ground, or at himself.

Skippy's lucky charm: The player chose it for him (Pirate background, PHB p.139): a small red coral horn charm on a cord around his neck. Red coral is a classic material for these charms. That is from memory, not from my web search.

At the table: he grips it when someone stares too long, praises him, or when the luck turns bad. Made up Idea for where it came from: a gift from someone on The Widow when he was small. "You don't buy your own. Somebody has to love you enough to give it." Ask your DM if that fits, or choose your own story.

The evil eye and how to lift it

Real world The evil eye is the belief that a look full of envy, or even a too-big compliment, can bring harm. Web sources describe a common way to find and lift it: put a few drops of olive oil in a bowl of water. If the oil spreads or breaks into small pieces, the evil eye is believed to be there. A prayer is said until the oil stays whole. Some families add salt or scissors. The water is often poured out afterwards. Prayers are often passed in secret from grandmother to a chosen family member, and they differ by place. These web sources are mostly blogs and family accounts, none only about Sicily. Treat this as folk practice, not a standard rite.

At the table: an NPC says "What a fine cannon!" Skippy touches iron and says "Bless it." Later, if the party has a run of bad luck, he drops three drops of oil in a cup of water and mutters. The DM may smile. Nothing more is needed. Made up No game effect unless your DM adds one.

Touch iron

Real world In Italy people touch iron, not wood, to push bad luck away. They do it right after they say something they hope will not happen. One web source says it comes from touching a horseshoe, long believed to scare off devils and witches.

At the table: this is Skippy's best one. Mourning Bell is black iron. Before he fires, he says "Touch iron" and knocks twice on the barrel with a knuckle. It sounds like a tiny bell.

Unlucky days: no wedding and no voyage on Friday or Tuesday

Real world An old saying goes: "Neither on Venus's day nor on Mars's day do you marry or set out." Venus's day is Friday. Mars's day is Tuesday. Both are seen as unlucky for a wedding or a journey. The exact wording changes from place to place. A Dartmouth folklore file agrees with this meaning.

At the table: the party plans to sail on a Tuesday. "Neither Venus's day nor Mars's day. Not today." Offer a deal: "Dawn tomorrow, and I'll hold iron all the way."

Bread never upside down

Real world It is bad luck to put a loaf of bread upside down. Web sources say it is Italian and European, not clearly Sicilian. Reasons given: bread is holy (a link to the body of Christ), and in fishing towns it was taken very seriously because bad luck could mean no fish. One web story (an executioner) is from a French blog, so I did not use it.

At the table: a friend puts the loaf on the table crust-side down. Skippy flips it quietly. "The sea knows." No speech.

The number 17

Real world In Italy the unlucky number is 17, not 13. Some hotels skip floor 17 and the Italian airline skips row 17. One web source says the Roman numerals for 17 can be re-arranged to spell "I have lived", meaning "I am dead". I found that in only one source, so it may be a modern story.

At the table: Skippy's Intelligence is 17. Joke: "Seventeen is unlucky. But it is my best number. Don't tell the sea." Never re-roll or ignore a real 17 on a die. The dice stay. The joke is in how you say it.

Sea and fishermen's customs

Tuna-net fishing and its work songs

Real world Sicilian fishermen used a big set of nets to trap tuna near the coast. A leader ran the whole work, and the final kill was called the "slaughter". Web sources say the men sang old work songs that set the rhythm of their work and asked God, the Virgin and the saints for a good catch. Some sources say the men should not look the leader in the eye. Today only a few remain, off the Egadi islands (Favignana is the famous one), because of over-fishing.

At the table: this is a great fit for "I hum the old songs of The Widow's crew". Skippy hums a call-and-response work song when he loads powder, readies the cannon, or digs. A friend may answer. Made up The song is only a rhythm: "Heave ... and hold ... fire ... and fall."

Saints of the sea

  • Saint Rosalia Real world is the patron saint of Palermo, the capital of Sicily. Tradition says the plague of 1624 ended after her relics were carried through the city. Her big festival is on 10-15 July; her church feast is 4 September. She is a city and plague saint more than a sea saint, so Skippy prays to her for the sick, not for the wind.
  • Saint Francis of Paola Real world is called patron of Italian seafarers (a pope named him in 1943). A legend says he once crossed the Strait of Messina by spreading his cloak on the water. It is a legend; the details differ by source. Skippy says his name when he crosses water.
  • Our Lady, Star of the Sea Real world (also "Our Lady of the Safe Harbour") is the Virgin Mary as protector of sailors. Wives of sailors left offerings in her church. I found this from one boating magazine, so the detail is light.
  • Painted eyes on the bow Real world A study says painted eyes on boat bows are a very old protection, as if the boat can see. Sicilian and Maltese boats still have them. At the table: he chalks two small eyes on the front of Mourning Bell. Made up "So it sees where it's going."

At the table: a quiet, serious beat: "Saint Rosalia, watch the sick. Saint Francis, watch the sea. And whoever watches captains, watch mine."

Two old sea sayings, in English

Real world Two Sicilian sayings, given here only in English: "Whoever sets out, gets there." (a call to take a chance) and "The sea gives and the sea takes." (do not get proud when luck is good). I checked these on web pages, not with a native speaker.

At the table: the first before a risk. The second, said softly, when he loses something or after a win.

6. How to roleplay Skippy

Voice, catchphrases, and a few simple rules to follow.

His voice

  • Short and clear. A powder monkey learned to speak so that people hear him over cannon noise.
  • Quick when he is excited, slow and quiet when he talks about the captain. The change tells the table something is under the surface.
  • Clever, and he knows it. A small proud smile when he explains how something works. Then he looks at a map and forgets you.
  • Plain English and a few sea words, nothing else. One sea word per scene is plenty. No foreign words are needed: the luck is in what he does.

Catchphrases

Pirate

"Aye, skipper." (to anyone he trusts)

"Fair winds, shipmate."

"Belay that. I've got a better idea."

"I gave my word. That's the end of it."

"Powder, shot, match. In that order."

Luck lines

"Touch iron." (before the cannon fires)

"Up anchor. The tide won't wait."

"Would you look at that."

"Whoever sets out, gets there." (before a risk)

"The sea gives and the sea takes." (after a loss)

Five ready-made table moments

Each one takes about 10 seconds. Ask your DM first for the ones that need the scene to match.

Salt spilled at the tavern

Set-up: a salt shaker tips over, or a friend knocks it.

Skippy: "Left shoulder. Quick." (He throws a pinch.) "Now the devil's blind. Eat."

Why it works: it is a tiny scene and nobody waits for you.

A black cat on the dock

Set-up: the DM describes a black cat crossing the party's path. (On a ship a cat is often lucky. In Italian luck, a black cat that crosses your path is bad.)

Skippy: "Wait. Let the cat go first. Cats know the way off a ship before we do." (He makes the horns sign low, pointed at the ground.)

Why it works: it makes the cat important. The DM may add a clue.

Before firing Mourning Bell

Set-up: any fight, when you activate the cannon.

Skippy: (swings Mourning Bell up onto his shoulder, or sets it down like a turret; knocks twice on the black iron; one hand closes on the coral horn at his neck) "Touch iron. Sing, girl." (It fires.)

Mourning Bell has no legs, so he always carries it or sets it down by hand. The knock is part of that. Keep it under three seconds. After the first time, just knock. Everyone will know.

A stranger praises him

Set-up: an NPC says "What a fine little cannon!" or "You're so brave, kid."

Skippy: (grips the coral horn charm at his neck) "Don't. ... Say 'God bless it' and touch the iron. Compliments bring the eye." (The NPC does it. He nods.)

Why it works: it shows his character and costs no rules. In many folk customs, you add a blessing when you praise.

Leaving on a Tuesday

Set-up: the party wants to leave at once, on the wrong day.

Skippy: "Not on Venus's day, not on Mars's day. Not to wed, not to go." (A pause.) "But the trail is warm. I'll go. I hold iron the whole road, and nobody whistles."

Why it works: he shows his luck rule and breaks it for the captain. That is the real story: his bond is stronger than his fear.

Bonus: a quiet moment Made up

When a friend or NPC dies, Skippy taps Mourning Bell once with his knuckle and says nothing. If your DM allows, do it once, for each loss. This is the best use of the cannon's name.

Keep it fun, not annoying

Do

  • One luck custom per scene, at most.
  • Ten seconds, then back to the game.
  • Let it make friends laugh. Invite them in ("Hold the iron. Quick!").
  • Let the dice stand. Customs are flavour, not rules.
  • Break a custom when it is for the captain. That shows who he is.
  • Tell your DM your plan. They may reward it with a clue.

Do not

  • Do not stop the party, or the fight, for a ritual.
  • Do not use a custom to refuse what the group voted to do. Make a deal instead.
  • Do not mock a real faith. Skippy believes. He does not joke at the saints.
  • Do not point the horns sign at people.
  • Do not speak in accent for a whole evening. It gets tiring for you and the table.
  • Do not turn a custom into a game advantage unless your DM says so.
Easy check: if a friend at the table is smiling, keep going. If they look at the clock, shorten it. Roleplay is not acting: you do not need a voice. Say, in your normal voice: "Skippy knocks on the cannon and says 'touch iron'." That is real roleplay.

7. How my magic looks

An artificer's magic looks like gadget tricks. Skippy does them with his tools or with his infused crossbow, which is also his spell focus.

Your books The rules leave the look of your spells to you (Tasha's p.11-12). Made up Everything below is an idea, not a rule. The spell works as the rules say; only the picture changes.

His tools

The thieves' tools roll holds: lockpicks, thin pliers, a small file, a little mirror and a hook.

Shocking Grasp

He touches the enemy with the steel tip of his pliers. A blue spark cracks, like a struck flint.

At the table: "Skippy jabs the pliers into the goblin. Sparks fly!"

Guidance

He tightens a friend's buckle, or taps their blade with a lockpick.

At the table: "There. Now it'll hold."

Shield

He snaps the crossbow up. Scratched marks on its stock flash, and a ring of iron light catches the hit.

At the table: "Skippy snaps the crossbow up. The marks flash. Clang!"

Thunderwave

He slams a pinch of black powder against the deck with his heel. It goes boom, like a small cannon shot.

At the table: "Skippy stamps his heel. Boom! Everyone back!"

Cure Wounds

A tar-smelling salve from a tin, then quick stitches with a sail needle.

At the table: "Hold still. This stinks, but it works."

Faerie Fire

He blows glowing "sea-sparkle" dust off his palm. It sticks to the enemies and makes them glow.

At the table: "Skippy blows on his palm. Now we can all see you!"

False Life

A gulp of bitter, smoky tonic from a hip flask, then a shiver.

At the table: "Skippy drinks, grimaces and shivers. He stands taller."

Detect Magic

He tilts the little mirror from his roll. Magic shows in it as a faint glow.

At the table: "He tilts the mirror. There. Something glows in that wall."

Magical Tinkering

He taps a coin with a lockpick. Now it shines like a candle, or whistles a tune.

At the table: "Skippy taps the coin. It glows like a little candle."
